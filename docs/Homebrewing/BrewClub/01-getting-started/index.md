# Getting started

Everything you need to know and do before brew day — how much you're brewing, what equipment you need, how to choose and share your sanitiser, and where you're going to ferment it.

None of this is complicated. But getting these decisions right before you buy anything means you'll spend money only on what you actually need, and your brew day will go smoothly.

---

## How much are you brewing?

The first decision is batch size, and it drives almost everything else: which fermenter, how to split the kit, what you're packaging into.

In BrewClub, the base unit is **4 litres** — enough to fill one Oxebar mini keg. Everything scales from there.

| People splitting the FWK | Batch size per person | Fermenter size needed |
|---|---|---|
| Solo | 10 L | 15 L |
| 2 | 10 L each | 15 L each |
| 3 | ~7 L each | 10 L each |
| 4 | 5 L each | 8 L each |
| 5 | 4 L each | 5 L each |

A standard FWK is concentrated to around 15–20 litres. Splitting between four people gives 5 litres each — one keg each, and manageable equipment. Splitting between two gives 10 litres each — two kegs each, or one keg and some bottles.

**Solo:** an extract kit pouch gives you enough for two batches. Brew one half now and store the other for later. 10 litres is a good starting size: two kegs' worth, and plenty to share and compare.

**Fermenter headspace:** your fermenter should be noticeably larger than your batch. Fermentation produces a foam layer (called krausen) that needs space — if the fermenter is too full, it can push out through the airlock. As a rule, leave at least 20% headspace: for a 5-litre batch, use an 8-litre or larger fermenter; for 10 litres, a 15-litre fermenter.

Choose your batch size, confirm your fermenter can handle it, then move on.

## The equipment you need

BrewClub is built around a short equipment list. Everything on it earns its place — not because it's the only way to brew, but because it keeps the process simple and the results reliable for a first brew.

### Fermenter

A polypropylene (PP) bucket fermenter with a tap, a lid, an airlock, and an EPDM grommet for the airlock.

Sizes: 5 L, 8 L, 10 L, 15 L, or 20 L depending on your batch. PP is the right material — food-safe, easy to clean, compatible with all sanitisers you'll use, and inexpensive. The tap lets you transfer to packaging without a siphon. The airlock lets CO₂ out while keeping air and contamination out.

:::tip Grommet matters
The grommet is the small rubber seal that holds the airlock in the lid. It should be black EPDM rubber, not red or orange. Black EPDM is chemically compatible with everything you'll use. If your fermenter comes with a red or orange grommet, replace it. Your equipment supplier can advise.
:::

### Airlock

A plastic airlock — a 3-piece or S-type design, both work. Fill the U-section with cooled boiled water or diluted sanitiser before fitting to the grommet.

### Sanitiser

See the [Sanitiser section](#sanitiser) below — it deserves its own explanation.

### Packaging

You have two options: **keg** or **bottles**. You don't need both.

**Keg (recommended for BrewClub):** an Oxebar 4-litre mini keg. Lightweight, portable, reusable. You can serve straight from it with a picnic tap, or add a mini regulator later for consistent carbonation. One keg per 4 litres of beer.

**Bottles:** PET bottles with carbonation caps, or glass bottles with crown caps. Bottles work well — they're flexible and shareable. They take a little more time to fill and seal than a keg, and natural carbonation means waiting two weeks before drinking. If you already own bottles, use them for Brew 1 and add a keg later.

:::tip You don't need both on your first brew
Pick one and stick with it. Kegging is slightly simpler on brew day — no filling multiple containers, no waiting for natural carbonation to complete. But if bottles are what you have, they're a perfectly good starting point.
:::

### Picnic tap (if kegging)

A basic picnic tap threads onto the Oxebar tapping head and lets you pour. No regulator needed to start — the residual CO₂ pressure in a carbonated keg is enough to serve from for the first few pours. A mini regulator is an easy upgrade once you've brewed once or twice.

### Thermometer

You need to know your wort temperature before pitching your yeast. An instant-read probe thermometer is ideal. A stick-on LCD strip thermometer on the fermenter is a workable alternative — accurate enough for pitching temperature and useful for monitoring fermentation.

### Scissors and a marker

For opening kits and yeast packets, and labelling your fermenter. Label it: brew name, date, yeast, and fermentation location. You'll thank yourself later.

### What you don't need for Brew 1

- A hydrometer or refractometer — FWKs have a known starting gravity, and fermentation time is a reliable enough guide to when you're done. Gravity measurement is introduced in Brew 2.
- A boiling kettle — a FWK or extract kit doesn't need a boil.
- A wort chiller.
- A pH meter.
- A pump or auto-siphon.

If you have some of these, they won't do any harm. But none of them are needed. The goal for Brew 1 is to learn the fundamentals with the minimum equipment possible.

## Sanitiser

Sanitising is the single most important thing you'll do on brew day. Any equipment that touches your wort or beer after the boil — and for Brew 1, that's everything — must be sanitised. Skipping or rushing this step is the main reason home brews occasionally don't turn out right.

The good news is that sanitising in BrewClub is genuinely simple.

### What sanitiser does

A sanitiser kills bacteria and wild yeast that would otherwise compete with your brewing yeast and ruin the beer. For a no-rinse sanitiser, the residual amount left on equipment after draining is too small to affect the flavour or safety of the finished beer — you don't rinse it off, you just let the equipment drain and brew straight into it.

### DES — the BrewClub sanitiser

For Brew 1, BrewClub uses **ChemPro DES** (or an equivalent ethanol-based sanitiser). It comes ready to use — no mixing, no dilution. Spray it on, let it drain, and brew.

DES is the right choice for a first brew for a few reasons:

- **No measuring.** ABNS sanitisers (the other common type, like StarSan or StellarSan) need to be diluted to the right concentration — too strong or too weak and they're less effective. DES is ready to use straight from the bottle.
- **No leftover diluted sanitiser.** With ABNS, you mix a batch and use what you need — which often means pouring the rest away. DES comes ready to use and stores indefinitely between sessions.
- **No foam.** ABNS creates foam, which is harmless but can look alarming the first time. DES doesn't foam at all.
- **Compatible with all BrewClub equipment.** DES is safe on PP, EPDM, and the other materials you'll encounter.

If you already own StarSan or StellarSan, use it — it works fine. DES is recommended for a first brew because it's simpler. The [Brew Day](../02-brew-day/index.md) page covers ABNS use as well.

### Sharing sanitiser in a club

Nobody needs to buy a full bottle of sanitiser for a single brew. The club leader provides one 5-litre bottle of ChemPro DES, which lasts across many sessions. On session day, each member fills a small spray bottle — 100 to 200 ml is plenty — directly from the club jug. Use it on the day, take the bottle home for next time.

A small spray bottle is cheap, and many people already have one. If you don't, ask at a pharmacy or supermarket.

### How much do you need?

For a 5-litre batch, 100 ml of DES is more than enough. For a 10-litre batch, 150 ml covers everything. The spray bottle is for coating surfaces — you're not soaking or filling, you're spraying and draining.

## Fermentation temperature

Find a space where a fermenter can sit undisturbed for one to two weeks, and get a rough sense of the temperature. This matters more than it might seem — your choice of yeast depends on it.

You don't need a thermometer or a precise reading. Just a general feel for the range:

| Temperature band | What it means |
|---|---|
| Below 15°C | Too cold for most ale yeasts — needs a specific cold-tolerant strain |
| 15–20°C | Standard ale yeast territory |
| 20–25°C | Still fine for most ale yeasts; kveik yeasts thrive here |
| Above 25°C | Kveik territory — standard ale yeasts may produce off-flavours |

**Where to look:** a corner of a room away from direct sunlight or radiators. Inside a wardrobe or cupboard. A garage or shed in the right season. Somewhere the temperature is reasonably stable is more important than hitting a specific number.

**If you're unsure:** err towards a cooler, stable spot rather than a warm one. Too cold will slow fermentation; too warm can produce unwanted flavours. Both are manageable, but stable is better than fluctuating.

The [Brew Day](../02-brew-day/index.md) page covers yeast selection — your temperature range is the key input to that decision. Have it in mind before you get there.

## FWK selection (Brew 1)

If you're brewing beer for Brew 1, you'll be starting with a fresh wort kit (FWK). A few things to look for:

**Choose a kit without liquid additions.** Some FWKs include liquid hop additions or other liquid ingredients alongside the concentrated wort. These are harder to split accurately between multiple brewers. For Brew 1, a kit that's wort-only (or includes dry hops, which are easy to divide) is the better choice.

**Avoid hazy IPA for Brew 1.** Hazy beers are very sensitive to oxygen — the style depends on keeping oxygen out from fermentation through to serving. The techniques for doing this reliably come with a little experience. Choose a pale ale, amber, stout, or a lager-style FWK for your first brew. Hazy is a great Brew 2 or 3.

**Talk to your supplier.** FWK availability varies by region and changes over time. Your local homebrew shop or online supplier can tell you what's currently available and what works well in a split. If your club leader has brewed from a particular FWK before, that's a good recommendation to follow.

**If an experienced brewer is providing wort:** this is the original BrewClub model — a brewer friend or local brewery provides a batch of fresh wort, which the club splits and ferments individually. Everything that follows is identical to the FWK path. If this is your situation, you don't need to source a kit at all.

## Before brew day — checklist

Go through this before brew day, not on it.

- [ ] Batch size confirmed
- [ ] Fermenter sized correctly for that batch (with headspace)
- [ ] FWK or extract kit sourced (or wort provider confirmed)
- [ ] Yeast chosen — based on your fermentation temperature
- [ ] Sanitiser ready — DES spray bottle filled, or ABNS if you have it
- [ ] Packaging ready — keg or bottles, enough for your batch size
- [ ] Fermentation location confirmed and temperature noted
- [ ] Thermometer available
- [ ] Scissors and marker pen ready

If all of these are in place, brew day is just following the steps. Head to [Brew Day](../02-brew-day/index.md) when you're ready.
