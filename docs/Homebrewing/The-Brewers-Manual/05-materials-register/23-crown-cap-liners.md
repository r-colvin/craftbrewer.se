# Crown caps and liners

Crown caps have two distinct components with two entirely different compatibility stories: the **cap body** (metal shell) and the **liner** (the polymer disc pressed against the bottle mouth). Most compatibility questions about crown caps are actually questions about the liner, but the cap body is worth understanding briefly.

## Crown cap bodies — tinplate or TFS steel

Crown cap bodies are stamped from either **electrolytic tinplate (ETP)** — low-carbon steel with an electrodeposited tin coating, typically 1–2 µm thick — or **tin-free steel (TFS/ECCS)**, which uses a thin chromium oxide layer instead of tin. Tinplate is the industry standard for beer crown caps; TFS is used where tin-free steel offers cost or lacquer-adhesion advantages. Both are widely used; the distinction rarely matters for homebrewing purposes.

Retailer descriptions are inconsistent. "Steel" (KegLand), "metal, corrosion-protected surface" (Ölkompaniet), and "stainless steel" (The Malt Miller) all appear in product listings for what are likely standard tinplate or TFS caps. Stainless steel crown caps exist but are a niche product — the industry standard is tinplate or TFS. If the distinction matters for your application, request a specification from the supplier.

Tin and chromium oxide are both electrochemically stable at the pH of beer (pH 3.8–4.5) and at the pH of working-dilution ABNS (pH 3.0–3.5). Neither is attacked by phosphoric acid or DDBSA at these concentrations and contact durations. Chlorine-based sanitisers or cleaners are a different matter — chloride ions can pit through tin or chromium oxide coatings at higher concentrations or prolonged contact — but ABNS products contain no chloride, and the chlorine-corrosion concern does not apply here.

In normal bottling practice, the cap body does not contact the beer directly — the liner bridges the gap between the metal rim and the glass bottle mouth. The cap body is a food-contact-adjacent material rather than a direct food-contact surface.

**Note:** Crown cap body material and food contact compliance is an area where primary manufacturer specifications have not been confirmed in this guide's research. The analysis above is based on the well-established chemistry of tinplate, TFS, and beer packaging. If you are sourcing caps from an unfamiliar supplier, request a Declaration of Conformity under [EU Regulation 1935/2004](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02004R1935-20090807).

## Crown cap liners — standard (non-O₂-scavenging)

The standard liner in modern PVC-free crown caps is **injection-moulded virgin polyethylene (PE)**, heat-moulded directly into the cap. This is confirmed by The Malt Miller's product description ("polyethylene heat moulded liner that is PVC free")[^1] and consistent with industry practice documented by closure manufacturers.[^2]

PE is chemically inert to ABNS, DES, phosphoric acid, and DDBSA at homebrewing concentrations. Standard crown caps with PE liners can be sanitised normally. Retailer descriptions typically confirm "PVC free" without specifying PE by name — this reflects the industry shift away from PVC plastisol, but does not represent a material gap: PVC-free crown cap liners are PE or equivalent polyolefin in practice.

Older caps, or caps from unfamiliar suppliers, may still use PVC plastisol liners. Plastisol is FDA-compliant and food-safe at beer contact conditions, but if you are sourcing caps with no material specification, "PVC free" is the minimum standard to request.

## Crown cap liners — oxygen-scavenging

| | ABNS | DES | Cleaning | Beer/wort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rating** | Avoid | Avoid | — | A |

*"Avoid" reflects functional deactivation of the liner's oxygen-scavenging chemistry, not structural damage or a safety concern. The cap seals correctly after liquid contact; the oxygen-scavenging capacity is destroyed.*

### How oxygen-scavenging liners work

Oxygen-scavenging crown cap liners — sold under trade names including DARAFORM® (The Malt Miller) — use a **moisture-activated chemistry** to scavenge oxygen from the bottle headspace after the cap is applied. The scavenging compound is most commonly an iron-based material (iron powder dispersed in a polymer carrier), though sodium sulfite-based formulations are also used in commercial brewing.[^3] When moisture contacts the liner, it activates the iron or sulfite compound, which then reacts irreversibly with oxygen.

The scavenging capacity is finite and single-use. Once activated, it cannot be restored.

### Why liquid sanitiser destroys the function

If the liner is wetted before capping — whether with water, ABNS, or any other liquid — the scavenging chemistry activates on contact with atmospheric oxygen from that moment. By the time the cap is applied to the bottle, the capacity has been partially or fully consumed doing nothing useful.

The cap seals correctly. There is no structural failure and no safety concern. But the oxygen-scavenging function that justified using these caps rather than standard caps is gone.

This matters in practice because brewers often sanitise all caps at the start of a bottling session, then apply them over 30–60 minutes. Later caps in the batch have had their chemistry depleted against ambient air rather than bottle headspace.

### The sanitiser question — ABNS specifically

The concern with oxygen-scavenging caps is **moisture activation**, not chemical attack by ABNS. Northern Brewer's product guidance explicitly states: "do not use an oxidizing cleaner or sanitizer such as One-Step or Easy Clean on caps" and identifies "Iodophor or Star San" as acceptable sanitisers when liquid sanitising is desired.[^4] The oxidising cleaner concern (One-Step, Easy Clean) is distinct from ABNS: these products are oxidising agents that could interact directly with the scavenging chemistry. ABNS (Star San, StellarSan) is not an oxidising cleaner in this sense — its phosphoric acid and DDBSA components do not oxidise iron.

However, ABNS is still a liquid. Wetting the liner with ABNS activates the moisture-dependent scavenging chemistry exactly as water would. The result is the same: premature depletion.

The practical guidance is therefore not "use Star San specifically" — it is **do not wet the liner at all**:

**Correct procedure for oxygen-scavenging caps:**
- Sanitise the bottles; do not sanitise the caps
- Apply caps directly from dry, sealed factory packaging
- Handle caps to avoid touching the liner surface
- Do not pre-soak or bulk-sanitise caps in advance
- If any cap has been wetted, treat it as a standard cap — the O₂-scavenging benefit is gone, but the cap still seals normally

Manufacturer cleanliness from sealed factory packaging is sufficient for the cap surface. The infection risk from a dry, factory-clean cap is negligible given that the bottle has been sanitised.

### Food contact status

Crown cap liners are [EU Regulation 1935/2004](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02004R1935-20090807) compliant food contact materials. The oxygen-scavenging chemistry (iron or sodium sulfite compounds in a polymer matrix) is approved for food contact in the quantities that migrate into beer headspace.
